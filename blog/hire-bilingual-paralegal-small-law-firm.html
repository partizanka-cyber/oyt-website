How to hire a bilingual paralegal for a small law firm

Outsource Your Tasks · November 21, 2026

If you own a small law firm, you are the lawyer and, most weeks, the person updating the file, requesting records, and calling the client back. When a Spanish-speaking caller reaches the office, that call often lands on you.

A bilingual paralegal carries the file work and the Spanish-speaking follow-up. You keep the legal judgment. This guide is for the owner who is ready to hire that person and wants a plain way to do it. Your jurisdiction sets the rules. One point covers the rest: a paralegal works under attorney supervision, and you stay the lawyer.

Decide which seat you are filling

Firms often post for a paralegal and then discover they needed two people. Separate the work first.

A paralegal supports matters that are already open. They organize the file, prepare drafts for your review, track deadlines, request records, and update the client with the status you approved. You review the work and you direct it.

A client intake coordinator answers the new lead, asks the screening questions you wrote, enters the caller in your system, and books the consult. If the phone is the problem and the files are in shape, start with intake. If the files and the calendar are the problem, start with the paralegal. Many firms add the second seat later. Both are described on our page for small law firms. The virtual paralegal role and the intake coordinator role go further on each seat.

Name the matters this person will touch. Family law, personal injury, and immigration reward different habits. Write four lines: the practice area, the first-month tasks, the software you already use, and the hours you need covered.

Say what bilingual means in your office. On a resume the word might mean childhood Spanish, or years of client calls in Spanish. You want someone comfortable in English with your staff and comfortable in Spanish with clients. Say which language the notes are in, and which language is used on the call.

A dedicated paralegal works only for your firm. If they need to be available while the phones are open, name the hours and the time zone. Latin America is a practical place to look for that overlap. Much of the region shares the business day with US offices, so a remote hire there can be working while you are.

What to hand over

Draw the line with the attorney who will supervise. Owners commonly pass across these tasks once that line is clear.

  • Building and maintaining the matter file.
  • Drafting routine letters and forms for your review before anything goes out.
  • Requesting records and tracking what has come back.
  • Keeping the deadline calendar and flagging a date while there is still time to act.
  • Scheduling clients, experts, and depositions.
  • Summarizing documents so you read the summary before the pile.
  • Calling clients with the update you approved, in English or in Spanish.
  • Getting a hearing file ready with time to spare.

You decide which tasks stay on the attorney's desk. A paralegal works under attorney supervision, so the review path should be clear from the first week. A strong paralegal pauses and asks when a task needs a lawyer's judgment. Treat that question as a sign of fit.

A list to take into the search

Fill this in before you read a single resume. It is also the right brief to bring to a strategy call.

  • Practice area, and the matters for month one.
  • Hours, time zone, and whether the seat is full time.
  • Which language is used on calls, and which language is used in the file.
  • The calendar, file, and client systems you already use.
  • Tasks the paralegal will own, and tasks that stay with the attorney.
  • Who reviews a draft before it goes out.
  • How a Spanish-speaking call is handled, and which questions come straight to you.

Signs you are ready

A large staff is not the test. Owners usually recognize one of these.

  • You finish the legal work and then stay late indexing records.
  • Deadlines live in your head, or in a notebook only you can read.
  • Spanish-speaking clients wait longer, because the person who can call them back is in a hearing.
  • New matters stall between "open a file" and a file that exists.
  • A general assistant still left you without someone who has worked inside a legal file.

What to listen for in the interview

Ask for one file they supported, from opening to a deadline they tracked. What did they prepare, who reviewed it, and what did they do when a date moved? A clear story shows you how they work.

Then hear both languages. Give them a short client call in Spanish. One situation is enough: the client wants to know if the records arrived, and the honest answer is that one provider has not sent them. Then ask for the same call in English. You are listening for facts, for a legal answer left with the attorney, and for English you can supervise.

Ask what they do when they do not know. You want a person who writes the question down and asks you before they tell the client.

Ask about the setup in plain terms. They need a private place for client conversations, a computer that can run your systems, and a connection that holds during your business hours.

Ask which practice areas they have actually supported. Years on civil files means they can learn your preferences. No time inside a file means the early months go to learning what a file is. For a small firm, that difference is why you are hiring a paralegal. Hiring for aptitude is reasonable when the practice is narrow and you have time to teach. Before the next call, write four lines.

  • Could I follow their English?
  • Did the Spanish role-play stay with the facts?
  • Did they describe a real matter and name who reviewed the work?
  • Did they know when to bring you a question?

In the office, or remote

Choose the person down the hall when the work is physical: courthouse filings, clients in the conference room all week, or a document that has to change hands in a meeting. Choose a remote bilingual paralegal when the work lives in the file and on the phone. You need overlapping hours, a secure login, and a lawyer reviewing the work. The question at the door becomes a check-in and a written list.

Some firms keep both: someone local for the courthouse, and a remote paralegal for the file and the Spanish-speaking follow-up. The remote hire works only for that firm and takes direction from the owner. Paying a person in another country can involve payroll steps for you and your advisors. Your attorneys still supervise the legal work.

The first month

Give them one practice area and one type of matter. Share the templates you trust, where files live, and a checklist. Walk through a closed file you liked and one open file they will support.

Agree on the review path. Drafts reach you before they go out. Client updates use wording you have approved. Deadlines are entered the day they arrive. You look at the calendar together once a week.

Write the Spanish path next to the English path: which calls they take, what they may say, and which questions come to you. The language changes who can have the conversation. Supervision stays with the attorney.

Introduce them as part of the team, and tell the current staff which tasks move. A short daily check for two weeks, then a weekly meeting, is enough. Use it for deadlines, saved questions, and one step in the process that is still clumsy.

Questions owners ask

Can they work entirely remotely?

Yes, when the work is in your systems and on the phone. Courthouse runs and in-person meetings still need someone local.

Do they have to live in my state?

How do I judge Spanish?

Hear a short role-play. Listen for a calm, accurate report that leaves legal answers with you.
